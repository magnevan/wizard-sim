Wizard Sim · art pipeline · design review

The Plan-to-3D Pipeline

How a place goes from a Python plan to a walkable, stylised 3D scene, as of courtyard plan revision 19, and the architecture we propose to move to. Part one describes what runs today and where it is weak. Part two proposes the changes, including what the terrain means for gameplay: navigation over terraces, and paths that players block with spells.

Part one: what runs today

The one-paragraph version

A place is authored once, as a plan: a Python script of 2D shapes with heights and materials. The plan is consumed two ways. The game rasterises it into a grid of cells that the deterministic sim walks on (heights, cliffs as walls, gates, markers), exactly as before the 3D work. The 3D view replays the same shape operations as exact polygons, derives typed edges (retaining walls, parapets, kerbs) from them, then decorates those edges and footprints with painted assets: Meshy models made from Codex pictures, and Codex-painted tiling textures. Simple geometry is built in code (ground polygons, wall faces, sweeps), but every surface you see comes from a painted source. Shots of the result go to a calibrated critic and to you.

Stages

1

The plan: one authored source

Input
A design read and a chosen layout (the structure and flow stages of making-a-place.md).
Output
A Plan object: a height grid, a material grid, gates, routes, the journey, landmarks, markers, and now an ordered log of shape ops.
Code
tools/place-plans/plan.py, plans/*.py, finish.py

Shapes are written in screen-aligned U,V units (1 u ≈ 0.6 m; a wizard is about 2.9 u tall). Operations are painted in order: fill (a polygon at a height and material), stairs (a polygon ramped along an axis, in 0.35 m treads), building (a footprint raised by its height), plus a few helpers. Later ops overwrite earlier ones, so order is the composition rule.

Finishing passes then edit the cells directly: parapet lips on drops over 1 m, closing each gate's line, woods on unreachable ground, woods belts at the map edge (the courtyard does several of these inline). These are numpy edits on the grid, not shape ops.

The rule we adopted today: fix shapes here, not downstream. Plans written for the grid relied on overlaps that were invisible in cells (a road strip that repaved "whatever was under it", landing rectangles poking into a curved road). In 3D those overlaps become visible notches. Revisions 18 and 19 of the courtyard rewrite roads, steps and flight heads as shapes that meet on purpose.

2

Cells for the game: unchanged contract

Input
The plan's grids.
Output
An LDtk level (terrain, height tiers, ground material, markers, props), compiled to rooms.gen.ts for the sim and grounds.gen.ts for the client.
Code
to_ldtk.py, then npm run content:build / content:check

The sim still walks on 0.5 u cells, though the plan's cells are 1 u (each becomes 2 × 2), so the staircase it draws is 1 u. A height difference over 0.5 m between neighbours is a cliff: the compiler emits it as a rock segment along the cell edges, and bodies, navigation and shots collide with segments, not with cells. Before anything reaches the game, the walk report checks the plan: one connected region holds every gate and landmark, no jumps inside flights, no unguarded drops beside routes, no reachable ground within sight of the world's edge, no blocking prop on a route. The 3D work changes none of this, and determinism is untouched because everything 3D is client-only.

3

The vector export: exact shapes for the 3D view

Input
The plan's shape-op log, plus its final cell grids.
Output
world3d-ground.json: disjoint regions (polygon, material, height), vertical faces, typed edge polylines, and a glitch report.
Code
tools/place-plans/vector.py (shapely)
  1. Replay. Each op is applied with polygon boolean ops in plan order, giving disjoint regions in world coordinates. Curves and diagonals stay exact instead of becoming 45° staircases.
  2. Reconcile with hand edits. Finishing passes edited cells, not shapes, so the replayed regions are compared with the final cells. Differences thicker than one cell become override regions, outlined from cells and rounded. Thin differences along curves are rasterisation noise and are ignored. Parapet lips and tiny blocks are dropped, since the 3D kit stands there instead.
  3. Hygiene. Each region is opened and closed by 0.3 u to remove hairlines. Leftover slivers go back to the region that owned them. This used to be a heavy-handed smoother; it is now deliberately small, because shape problems are fixed in the plan.
  4. Stairs are cut into tread polygons along their axis.
  5. Faces. Every region edge that stands above its neighbour emits a vertical face, from its top to the neighbour's height.
  6. Typed edges. Walkable edges are classified and chained into polylines. A drop at the edge of walkable ground is a retaining wall; a paved edge over a drop above 1 m is a parapet; two different materials meeting, at least one of them paved, is a kerb (so road against grass gets one; never on a flight, never between dais, plaza and paving). Edges that still turn more than a 2 u radius curve would are smoothed and reported.
4

Kit placement: assets on the plan

Input
The plan and the ground export.
Output
world3d-place.json: about 360 placed pieces (kit id, position, ground height, facing, width, optional height).
Code
tools/place-plans/spike3d_place.py

Placement comes in three kinds, from most to least principled:

  • Along exact edges (parapets). Each parapet polyline is cut into chords about 3.4 u long, with a joint forced at turns sharper than 30°. A balustrade is stretched to fill each chord exactly, with piers at the ends, at sharp turns and at every second joint, and every fourth pier (counted across the whole courtyard, not per parapet) a lantern. This fits any curve or corner.
  • Along cell edges (building fronts). Wall modules are laid round each building footprint by walking its edge cells in runs of one direction. This is the older method, and still how non-hero buildings are dressed.
  • By hand, from the plan's geometry (hero pieces). The great hall's gateway, wall modules along its arc, its piers, planters and cypresses are placed at polar coordinates matching the plan's hall.

A hygiene pass then moves dressing that sits within 0.9 u (plus half its width) of an edge or off paving or grass, or drops it. In practice it currently removes every bench and urn, and the hand-placed lanterns are deleted outright in favour of the parapet ones. The old cell-based balustrade and retaining-wall code is still in the script but disabled.

5

Assets: painted sources only

Models
Codex isolates each piece from the concept as a front-on, orthographic picture on plain grey (the Meshy agent's prompt pattern). Meshy image-to-3D (meshy-7.1, PBR maps, remeshed to about 10–30k triangles) turns it into a model. About 30 credits a piece; 405 spent so far.
Textures
Codex paints seamless squares (flagstone, cobble, grass, ashlar, coping, slate) in the concept's palette; tile3d.py blends each with its half-shifted self so it tiles.
Rule
No art drawn in code. Geometry may be built in code (ground, faces, sweeps, flat roofs), provided its surface is painted. You approved this distinction on 2026-11-23, after rejecting code-drawn pixel art.

Every generated source is kept under game/content/generated/, with its prompt under tools/building-art/prompts/.

6

The renderer: a separate page, for now

Code
game/packages/client/world3d.html + src/lab/world3d.ts (three.js, already a client dependency for the 3D characters)
Camera
Perspective, 28° field of view, pitched 46°, framed to show 18 u of ground, leading 3.5 u up the screen so buildings ahead stay in frame. Chosen to match Magicka's steep, close view.
Light
A warm, low sun with 4096 px shadow maps; a hemisphere fill; a faint room environment for the PBR materials; ambient occlusion (GTAO); ACES tone mapping.

The ground is built from the export: region polygons are triangulated and textured by material, faces become vertical quads in ashlar, and edges get swept profiles (a coping along retaining walls and parapets, a low kerb along material joins). Building footprints become flat slate roofs at one fixed eave height (3.4 m above their ground) whatever the plan's building height, so a 15 m bell tower is as tall as a shed. Each region also gets a slightly larger copy 0.15 u underneath, to hide hairline gaps. Each kit piece is a clone of its GLB, scaled to its placed width (and height, for pieces that carry one). The wizard is his own animated GLB; movement in the spike uses a simple step check (0.6 m, against the sim's 0.5) on the level's height grid, not the sim.

Evaluation: shots, a critic, and you

npm run shot:world3d renders fixed spots headless (software GL) at 1920 × 1080. A beauty critic follows docs/campaign/beauty-rubric.md: full-size frames only; anchors you have rated, with your notes; hard caps (mixed scale, mixed viewpoint, inconsistent light, code-drawn art, the wrong camera); a greyscale readability test; then a score plus a separate craft score. It is never told what changed or what the last round scored, because that is what inflated it before. A glitch report from the export catches some geometric faults mechanically. The final check is your eye; your circled screenshot found faults that neither caught.

What the outside review found

A separate reviewer read this page against the code. Its points that change the picture above:

  • The picture and the collision differ by design, not by half a cell. Parapet lips are 1 u blocking cells in the sim, but the view drops them and puts a thin balustrade inside the edge: an invisible wall about 1 u deep in front of every parapet. Small buildings (gate piers, threshold pillars, 5–8 u²) block their whole footprint in the sim but show as paving with a narrower pier. In a game where shots and knockback hit walls, this is a gameplay bug.
  • The reconcile diff isn't a check: stairs and buildings are accepted without comparing heights, thin differences are erased, and the count is printed, never asserted. The spike's wizard walks the level's heights while the ground comes from a committed JSON, with nothing tying them to the same plan revision.
  • The pipeline is manual, three scripts run by hand in order with no staleness check, and the placement script hard-codes the courtyard and its hall.
  • Asset weight (about 90 MB for one place) doesn't fit a web build deployed on every push.
  • Its recommendations: an agreement check that fails the build; finishing passes as shape ops so the override path goes; one command for the chain; run the yard through untouched before anything else; then performance (compression, instancing, a laptop measurement); only then the building kit. Roofs belong on the painted side of the line. Heroes are whole models whose footprint the plan takes from the model. Adoption needs a frame-time target, about 15 MB per place, a draw-call count, the time and credits for a second place, and zero cell/polygon mismatches.

Part two: the proposed architecture

The spine: shapes are the only truth

Today the plan is a script that paints cells and also logs shapes, and later passes edit the cells directly. Two things are derived (the sim's cells and the view's polygons) and they disagree in ways nothing checks. The proposal:

  1. Every plan edit is a shape op, including today's finishing passes: parapets become an edge op along a polyline, woods belts and unreachable ground become fills computed as polygons, gate closures become short wall ops. No numpy edits in plan specs. The override and rounding path in vector.py goes.
  2. One compile step turns the ops into a resolved scene: disjoint regions with heights, typed edges, and solid features (parapets, piers, walls, building footprints), each with an exact outline.
  3. Collision comes from outlines as segments; cells keep heights and navigation only. The sim already collides bodies, shots and navigation with wall segments (w.terrain: the body push-out and clipRay in walls.ts, and nav.ts). Rasterising a 0.3 u balustrade into 0.5 u cells would either lose it or fatten it back into the invisible wall we are removing. So the compiler emits collision segments straight from the resolved outlines: a parapet blocks exactly where its balustrade stands, a curved retaining wall is a smooth chain of segments, not a staircase. Cells carry the height grid and the navigation grid. This changes the golden hashes, on purpose, in its own commit.
  4. The permanent agreement check is drawn against blocking: the placed pieces' real footprints (placement data plus model bounds) and the drawn edges, compared with the collision segments and the walkable cells. Anything drawn as solid must block; nothing invisible may block, within a stated tolerance (about 0.15 u), using each model's traced footprint rather than its bounding box. It fails content:check. A cells-against-scene comparison is only a transitional tool while plans still carry numpy edits.
  5. One command with a stamp: plan → resolved scene → level, 3D ground and placement, all stamped with the plan's hash and the hashes of the models whose footprints it uses. A mismatch fails content:check and trips a development-only assert; a deployed client never refuses to draw.
  6. Reproducible builds: the compiled level is committed and never regenerated on a client. The geometry library is pinned, since its output can change between versions.

Hygiene stays only as a guard: a sliver or notch the plan's ops leave is a compile error naming its position, not something to smooth away.

Assets: kits fit, heroes define their footprint

  • No non-uniform stretching. A run is filled with whole modules plus at most one filler, and the plan snaps lengths to whole bays. Balustrades become module and post pairs at a fixed pitch.
  • Building kit (bay, corner, gable end, roof run, roof corner), trimmed and pivoted in Blender so pieces tile, with heights from the plan's building height.
  • Hero buildings are whole models; the plan imports their footprint, traced from the model, instead of stretching the model to the plan.
  • Roofs move to the painted side: roof modules from the kit, never a flat polygon.
  • Budget: about 15 MB per place after Draco or meshopt geometry and KTX2 textures; repeated pieces instanced.

Part three: gameplay on terrain

Two rules keep this safe under rollback netcode. The sim never reads the 3D view: everything is computed from the resolved scene at build time or from sim state at run time, with integer costs. And static data stays out of the snapshot: anything baked at build time is read-only and outside buffers(); only small dynamic state is saved each tick.

Navigation: why today's won't scale

Today each seat has a Dijkstra distance field over 0.5 u cells, rebuilt every 0.3 s, stored in world state at MAX_SEATS × MAX_CELLS 32-bit integers. The courtyard level is 261 × 260 u, about 271,000 cells. The larger cost is the snapshot, not the search: four fields of that size are several megabytes saved every tick for rollback. And a resimulation that crosses a rebuild tick runs the rebuild again, so rebuilds have to be budgeted per rollback, not per 0.3 s. Issue #17 tracks this; it exists with or without 3D.

Navigation: proposed

  1. A region graph, baked at compile time and kept out of the snapshot. Nodes are walkable areas; edges are the passages between them (flights, gates, openings), each with its cells and width. The compiler splits regions at narrow passages so the obvious chokes are edges.
  2. Baked portal fields. For each portal, a static distance field over its neighbouring regions, computed at build time. An enemy far from the fight follows these downhill toward the next portal on its route, so it steers round the armillary and piers inside a large region without any runtime search. Free under rollback, since nothing is saved.
  3. One fine window per seat near the fight. Each seat's field covers a window around its wizard, wider than wall-casting range plus the longest wall (place range is 9 u; the longest drawn wall is to be cited from config), so every fresh wall lands inside some seat's fine field. Players can chain up to 40 walls each across any width, so no region split can bound what they close; the windows can. Walls left behind outside every window are handled by bump-and-break: an enemy that meets one breaks it at its break cost. its origin snapped to a coarse grid with hysteresis so it doesn't thrash, and stored in state. The window's border takes its costs from the graph distances, so a path crosses the border without a jump. Window fields are small enough to snapshot.
  4. In the wizard's own region but outside the window (the plaza is wider than a window), there is no next portal. The rule: steer by the baked field of the portal nearest the wizard until the window is reached, then by the window's field.
  5. Rebuilds are staggered across seats and ticks, and triggered only from sim state (a wall's creation or expiry tick, or the existing timer), never from anything outside the sim.
  6. Each enemy stores its chosen next portal, and ties break by portal index, so packs don't oscillate between two equal routes.

Spells that block paths

  • Closure is a connectivity test, not an overlap. A wall covering part of a wide flight leaves it open. When a solid wall or barrier touches a portal, the portal's cells are tested for a way across; only then is the edge closed or costed.
  • One cost model for blocked routes. Solid walls get an integer break cost on the edge, as players' force fields already have. One Dijkstra over the region graph (tens of nodes) then decides between going round and breaking through. No separate "detour budget".
  • Only what changed is recomputed: the touched portals' state and the affected windows. The dynamic portal state is a few bytes per portal in the snapshot.
  • A plan rule: every fight area has at least two routes in, or its single choke is deliberate and named in the read. The walk report counts routes per region and flags single points of failure.
  • Walls on terrain: a tier is defined by step connectivity (neighbours within 0.5 m), so a flight is one tier. A wall spell is clipped to the tier it is cast on (the aim point's tier for ranged casts, to be decided), never floating over a drop or buried in a cliff, and its preview shows the clipped length before it lands.

Other gameplay effects of height

  • Shots and edges: "shots pass over an edge from above, hit the face from below" needs shots to carry a height in state and cliffs to become directional segments. That is real sim work, not a given. Sight should stay symmetric, so enemies on a terrace don't get a free shot you can't return, with cover only close under an edge.
  • Knockback near drops: today every public edge over 1 m has a parapet. Leaving some open, so knockback can push someone off, needs a falling state for bodies. Out of scope until decided.
  • Jumping down: one-way links for enemies are cheap in the graph but need the same falling state. Left out of the first navigation step.
  • Readability: what blocks must look like it blocks. That is what the agreement check protects; it's a gameplay requirement, not polish.

Questions worth your review

  • Which laptop, and what frame rate, is the adoption bar? Is about 15 MB per place acceptable?
  • For a ranged wall spell, should it clip to the caster's tier or the aim point's?
  • Should any drops be open, so knockback can push someone off? That needs a falling state, and so would enemies jumping down.
  • Is "two routes into every fight area, or a named choke" the right rule for plans?
  • Should steps 1 to 5 (the compiler, collision from outlines, navigation) go ahead on main now, ahead of the art decision? They change the golden hashes once, on purpose.
