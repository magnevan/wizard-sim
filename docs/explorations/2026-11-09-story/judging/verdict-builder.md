# Verdict: the builder

| | S | Q | P | R |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buildable by a tiny team | 7 | 3 | 5 | 3 |
| Fit with spell language and pillars | 8 | 7 | 7 | 7 |
| Reuse of systems and regions | 8 | 7 | 7 | 4 |
| Co-op and solo robustness | 8 | 6 | 7 | 5 |
| Resilience to cutting a third | 8 | 5 | 6 | 2 |
| **Total** | **39** | **28** | **32** | **21** |

**Ranking: S, P, Q, R.**

I judged from the plans only and did not check how capable the enemy-caster AI is today. S depends on that most.

## S: Your Go

**Most expensive:** four rival casters who must be fun to lose to, grow every region, and stay readable beside up to four players with friendly fire on. It is one investment but a single point of failure. If the Dunnocks play badly, the game has no spine.

**Cleverest:** the slate. One number per town, one line per party size, retaken on the same ground, turns every shipped map into endgame content for a few integers of save data. The spar rule is nearly as good: rivals match your headcount and shortfalls become structures, never health, so party size is solved by a rule. There is no branching. The copybook puts the plot inside the spell language, and pillar 1 is the premise.

## P: Before All the People

**Most expensive:** the ending matrix. Seven oaths, three records, two wordings and two verdicts, sung verse by verse and shown at every gate, on top of two forks whose sides are bespoke. Each path through Shushan's day is a one-off most players see once.

**Cleverest:** oaths as rule-kits on existing shrines. "No Fire past the ford, if anyone saw it burning" is a small check in a deterministic sim that reaches straight into the hands. The rune left on the dyke that you lack in the duel is pillar 7 made moral. Small oaths scale freely, but the great oaths interlock, and forbidden elements press on pillar 6.

## Q: The Flitting Year

**Most expensive:** the finale, eleven miles of moving city with a device per job and a mile-long animal, which has no ancestor elsewhere in the game. Behind it sit about 36 jobs with salvage variants, and seven seats each needing bare and resettled palettes and enemy sets.

**Cleverest:** one moving front, a single sim feature that drives every leg and re-dresses each map so returning is automatically different. A bare post removes an option and adds no enemies, which is excellent scope discipline. But the plot is welded to the seat table, so moving the map means redoing the rotation.

## R: What He Had on Him

**Most expensive:** the prop matrix. Any spell on any prop, cast changing the answer, three routes per key prop, destroyable evidence and witness fallbacks, all bespoke and proofed against a logic chain one inconsistency breaks.

**Cleverest:** depth as information. A steadier Fire reads more, so ordinary progression reopens old rooms. The Lock Street Men are smart economy too: bad casters are cheap AI and good comedy. But every land carries an exhibit the deduction needs, so it cuts worst. It is spent after one playthrough, and in a party someone reads while the others wait or burn the evidence.

## What I'd cut first from S

The bell fight's free simulation: shoving three tons of bronze by spells, and the Walk that names who last damaged each house. Make it a scripted tug-of-war over zones with authored destruction. The story only needs the bell to sink and the Pargeters' house to burn.

Then, to reach a full third, shrink the great tree to a short stop, since Cullet is already the escort, and drop the bridge's per-span new arts to two. Keep the Dunnocks, the slates, Twice and the last cast.
