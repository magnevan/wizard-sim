# Level-design principles

The checklist for [making a place](making-a-place.md): the read, the plans and the critics all use it. It's a living page, so add a principle when a place teaches one. Facts about other games are from memory and *(unverified)* unless they're linked.

## Shape

1. **The outline says what the place is.** A hub is round or many-sided and faces inward. A road is a ribbon that bends. A fort is a ring with a weak spot. A rectangle is a placeholder, never the answer.
2. **Every place is part of somewhere.** Show what lies past the edges: a cliff dropping to the sea, a roofline, a road leaving, a tower on the next hill. A room that ends at a hard line feels like a box. *Baldur's Gate 3* plans its areas as sprawling chunks of the world, with their edges shaped by terrain, not walls *(unverified)*.
3. **Height before props.** Two or three tiers joined by stairs and ramps give a place more interest than any amount of dressing. You get the upper tier as a balcony, the lower as a stage, and a reason to climb. Walls along a tier edge double as places to look down from.
4. **Edges have a cause.** A cliff, a sea wall, a building's face, a hedge. There's always something real there, and you can see why you can't go further.
5. **A scale ladder.** A big landmark, a few middling buildings, many small things. When everything is the same middling size, the place looks flat.

## Flow

6. **A landmark you can see before you can reach it.** From each entrance you see the place's landmark (or "weenie", the Disneyland term), and the main route bends towards it. In *Hades* the exit reward shows on the door before you reach it *(unverified)*.
7. **Route hierarchy.** The main route is the widest, the brightest, and paved with the best material. Side routes are narrower, darker or rougher. When paths show it, signs aren't needed.
8. **Light leads.** Lamps, a glowing well, a sunlit opening. The eye goes to the brightest warm spot, so put it where the player should go next.
9. **Framing at thresholds.** Entering through a gate, an arch or between two obelisks frames the view of what comes next. The first frame after a door is a composed picture.
10. **Reward the detour.** A dead end holds something: a view, a bench by the cliff, a secret, a rune shard. *Tunic* hides routes behind what the camera can't see *(unverified)*, which we can use with tiers and roofs.
11. **Rest and look.** A hub has places to stop: a balustrade over the sea, a bench under a tree. They tell the player there's no rush here.

## Sense

12. **Built by someone, for a reason.** Paths go between doors. Benches face something. Planters hold plants, and paving is laid round them. Wear shows on the routes people use.
13. **Materials meet properly.** Paving gets a kerb or edge where it meets grass, grass thins into dirt at a path, and water meets a quay, a shore or a cliff. The joint is where a place looks either real or fake.
14. **Water has depth.** It shows a shore band of foam or ripples, darker water further out, and the cliff or quay above it casting shade. Water sits lower than land.

## In our camera

15. **The camera sees north-west faces less.** Rooms grow towards the south-east, so place the grand view where the camera faces it. The view figures are in [the opening's space diagram](01-opening/space.md).
16. **Tall things in the south hide the place.** Buildings along the southern edge cover what's behind them, so keep the south low and open (a terrace, a cliff edge, a low wall) or cut it away.
17. **The landmark sits a little north of where you arrive,** so it's in view straight away and you walk towards it.
