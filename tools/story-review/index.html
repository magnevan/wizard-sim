Four Roads Round the Sea

Four stories for the journeyman's circuit, each revised three times with its own editors. Three judges read them blind and picked one.

Options for you to react to, none of it canon. Names and places are placeholders on a stand-in map.

The four at a glance

Plain summaries, spoilers included. The full stories are further down if you want them.

Your Go

The idea
A summer of friendly competition. A rival group from another school does the circuit alongside you, and you keep sparring with them.
Main system
The rivals. Any spell that hits you is added to your spellbook, so losing teaches you their tricks. Each town also keeps a scoreboard you can retake.
Who opposes you
Nobody. The rivals are friends, and each land just has a local problem.
Role of the wells
Small. Attuning is a local job on the way to the next spar.
Tone
Comic and warm, often goofy. It turns bittersweet as the rivals leave one by one. A burned house at midsummer is the one real loss.
Ending
The last rival leaves. You finish a bridge between the two schools, fight one monster, and cast a spell she left you, which throws you in the harbour as a parting joke.

The Flitting Year

The idea
Every 211 years, on a known date, each land's magic moves on to the next land round the sea. Whole cities have to move with it, and the circuit exists to train people for this.
Main system
A calendar of jobs. Two always fall due together, so you lose one. A moving line crosses fights, with your rune regen different on each side, and each land changes element between visits.
Who opposes you
The date itself. No villain, only frightened people and huge animals that follow the gap.
Role of the wells
Large. Attuning at a well lets you steer the moving line, which decides what gets saved.
Tone
Grand and serious, with crowd humour. Hundreds die at the midpoint whatever you do.
Ending
You defend an eleven-mile floating city on the move. The result is a count of who made it, shown as lamps on a map.

Before All the People

The idea
To attune at a well, you swear a practical promise to the people living there. You keep coming back to pay those debts, and eventually they collide.
Main system
Your record of promises kept, bent or broken, which opens or shuts towns. Some promises restrict your spells, such as no Fire past a certain ford.
Who opposes you
Nobody evil. Two peoples with fair claims on the same land, and a classmate who promised both sides.
Role of the wells
The centre of everything. The promise is the price of attuning.
Tone
Solemn and moving, with a few bright moments, like a wedding where setting your friends on fire is good manners.
Ending
A great migration forces you to choose a side in a final battle on the ice. Then every promise you made is sung back to the people you made it to.

What He Had on Him

The idea
A murder mystery. An old man washes up with seven objects, one from each land, and you travel to all of them to find out who he was.
Main system
Spells read evidence: frost shows where hands gripped, Fire relights burnt pages. Each new element opens clues you walked past before.
Who opposes you
One person with an understandable secret, plus a few angry locals.
Role of the wells
Small. They give power, while locals teach the clever tricks.
Tone
Dry detective story, with some real sadness.
Ending
You hold a sea gate against one great surge, then decide what goes on the record about who was to blame.

The winner: Your Go

  • Its main problem for you. The creator judge's sharpest note: "the wells barely matter". Attuning is a job on the way to the next spar, and you said attuning was the part you liked. See question 2.

Your Go, in full

Two tiny schools joined by one old timber bridge send their journeymen round the wells in the same summer, and you spend it trying to beat four people who are cleverer than you, funnier than you, and all going to leave.

The question

Are they better than you? You'll ask it on the very first afternoon, when a total stranger flattens you with a spell you could have cast yourself, and from then on it's chalked inside the front cover of your copybook, in her handwriting: Bet 1, You 0. That score never resets and it counts every try. Win a spar early, and you can, and she'll write it down with an asterisk ("I was eating", "you didn't mean it"). The answer, I'm so sorry and so happy to tell you, is yes, by about fifty to one with a great many asterisks, and the one clean win is the best day of your life and also the reason the summer ends.

What drags you through a forty-hour game when the world is completely fine:

  • The slates. Each cohort chalks its names, its finishing queue, and whatever that town actually cares about (goose health, cracks, friends struck), one line per party size. A re-sit is next week's job on the same ground, and the Dunnocks keep re-sitting behind your back, even after they've stopped travelling: Hob's new scores arrive by post. Bet will retake a line the day after you beat it, and Ines goes over your chalk in tidier handwriting, in front of everyone, in blue.
  • The copybook. Any queue that touches you, anywhere, friend or foe, writes itself in, so every loss teaches you one more of her tricks and anyone can get there in the end.
  • The table. Each region ends at one long supper, one line each, loser lays it, and somebody does your walk, your wince and your exact face the last time you set fire to yourself. There's a chair fewer as the summer goes on.
  • The letter. Captain Sloe has been carrying one for Bet since the first week, and every port she holds it out, and every port Bet says "not yet" and comes to find you for another spar.

The opening

Last-Day Fête at Pennick, on the ochre peninsula in the northern bay, over an Arcane vein, with the Dunnock Point school across the strait at the far end of the Long Walk, a three-hundred-year-old street of timber houses on piles. Every stall is an element with one rune each: freeze the duck pond, soak the mayor without soaking the queue, and Mr Sedge etches your focus so you can conjure your very first blade. Any stall can be skipped. At the coconut shy Bet casts one colossal five-rune spell, obliterates the shy, and stands there grinning for one entire minute while her runes come back, and you decide you're going to get her. There's only one ferry out, so off you all go together.

The road

The route round the sea is fixed, with side paths free on every return, and after midsummer every leg runs back through the harbour. Each attunement steps your regen and deepens the pool you pick, each re-sit you win deepens one more, and attuning means working a place until your casting picks up its accent, on a job set by the locals.

The spar rule: as many Dunnocks step up as there are of you, and if they run short, what they've built before you arrived makes up the difference, more turrets, more glyphs, never more health. In joint fights they rotate in by the wave, so you always have someone to hit by accident and the screen stays readable.

1. Saltcrown (north-centre mesas, Earth)
  • The place: a fortress so old and rich that its ramparts have ramparts, sitting on a red mesa you can see for two days before you reach it.
  • The trouble: scarp bulls have discovered that the mortar is salty. They are licking the fortress. A duke's east wing is noticeably thinner and he's in it.
  • Asks and gains: hold the salt gate with conjured rock walls, your first new verb, and the slate counts stones licked.
  • Encounters: defence, herds, native boss, first spars.
  • The beat: the Dunnocks have already passed the exam, by accident, looking for lunch, and their line is outrageously good.
  • On return: the herd has told its friends, and Lightning opens the sealed quarry.
2. The great tree (south-west, Life)
  • The place: the tree is so enormous that noon comes to the village of Windfall for twenty minutes through one gap in the leaves, and everyone stops work and goes and stands in it. They live on what drops and legislate who owns a bough while it's still in the air.
  • The trouble: a bumper year, and boars are eating the festival.
  • Asks and gains: escort the prize cart and its hateful goose through the woods alive, and the slate counts goose. Wards on an ally come from Hob, who walks beside you healing you through your own fire and apologising to you for the fire.
  • Encounters: escort, swarms, a keeping shrine.
  • On return: Hob's oven with a queue round the root, and a spar against months of strange Life spells.
3. The Stirrup and Cullet (south-west badlands into the volcanic south, Fire)
  • The place: the great rock arch where both cohorts camp, and then the mountain, which spits molten glass that cools in the air and comes down at dawn as bubbles over a black land split with fire, and the children of Cullet run out with nets.
  • The trouble: Cullet has staked everything on the largest pane of glass ever made, and it must go down the lava road on a cart.
  • Asks and gains: an escort where your own rocks and shoves break the thing as fast as anything else, so you learn the parry from Bet, batting fire away from a window while she criticises your elbow. The slate counts cracks.
  • Encounters: the first joint defence at the arch, friendly fire in both directions, then ambushes. The black spires hold constraint shrines.
  • The beat: supper under the arch, where Hob, apologising, announces he's been asked to stay at Windfall and everyone screams with joy. First empty chair. Next morning Dov plants a turret and walks away from it, it shoots you in the back, and you have never wanted anything so much.
  • On return: one tiny pane in Cullet has your names in it, slightly cracked.
4. Longlegs (north-east fen, across the sea on Sloe's ferry, Water)
  • The place: a town on stilts in a storm marsh, washing strung between chimneys, and after every storm the marsh gas lights itself and walks about on the water at night in tall blue flames.
  • The trouble: the flames have started drifting in under the boards.
  • Asks and gains: the Douse Round, running the walkways putting out fire with Water, and the slate counts boards scorched.
  • Encounters: ambushes, defence, amphibians, puzzle rooms in the sunk houses.
  • The Sigil art opens here. A storm pins everyone in a sail loft for three short, skippable days. Dov's queue sits greyed in your copybook and your etched focus can't do it, so Dov takes the focus, Mr Sedge's own work, walks out to the rail, and you hear the splash. You will be furious. You try the queue at the dummies, it fizzles, it fizzles, and on the third morning the cast you press works and a blade stands in the air by the window with no hand on it. You could only shape what you hold; now you shape the world around you. Opening the art grants Sigil depth outright. Your first turret takes out the last chapter of Captain Sloe's novel.
  • On return: next week's flames, and Longlegs has hung your focus's empty case in the loft as a warning.
5. The Ribs (north, Cold)
  • The place: the ribs stand out of the ice taller than any tower you've seen, clouds snag on them, and you will not want to be among them at night. The monks of the Copying House live in the skull, transcribing every well's slate, and their oldest book records it plainly: it came up out of the north, lay down here, took nine winters to die, and the first monks sat with it in shifts.
  • The trouble: armoured things are walking in from everywhere to lie down among the ribs, straight over the sledge line, and they don't stop for sledges.
  • Asks and gains: freeze a lead of open sea and run the winter stores round them, past the largest, whom the monks call the Prioress. It's the same ice that brings timber home for the bridge. The monks, you discover, have started a rota for the armoured things too.
  • Encounters: gauntlet, caravan, boss, traversal over water you froze.
  • The beat: in the copied slates are two hundred years of paired cohorts, and there's Mr Sedge's name beside Mrs Ruddock's, the Dunnock teacher. Then the gossip: Ines says she's here to count the ribs, which won't come out even, but it's the bolt she's been working on, and she has it, and Bet tells her to leave it alone, and you hear a Dunnock shout for the first time. In the morning her chair is empty and nobody does your walk.
  • On return: she never looks at you. Her spars open on the answer to whatever element you've cast most. The shelves hold a copy of Sloe's novel.
6. Twice (north-west island, Lightning)
  • The place: the blue crystal peak rings like a struck glass with every bolt, and each house in the lamp-makers' town proudly paints its hits on the wall.
  • The trouble: one great double strike each year fills every lamp on the island for winter, and someone must be standing on the catch-stone for the second bolt.
  • Asks and gains: climb through swarms that chain, with lamp-carriers roped along who count as friends, and charge the coil line with your own spells. The slate counts friends struck. Bet's line is a zero. Blink, for the ledges.
  • Encounters: gauntlet, chain swarms, coils, and the top.
  • On return: the lamps are lit from what you caught, and they're very proud of you and a little scorched.
7. The Rucks (east, Arcane)

You go alone now, and I'll keep this short.

  • The place: purple hills in a different order each morning, stars out at noon, and the tower floating above it all, its great east window missing for years and things coming in through the hole at night. Mrs Aumbry has held that room with a curtain and a terrible temper. Catch one of the things and it's small, and lost.
  • Asks and gains: the Cullet pane arrives and you fit it through a night of intruders. Attune becomes how you fight here, flooding your pools between waves. The largest regen step. If a tenth element comes in, Air blows through that hole.
  • Encounters: intruder defence, reshuffled rooms, strange lone creatures.
  • The exam: Arcane draws out what's already in a caster, so the well room fills with Bet and Hob and Ines and Dov, drawn out of your copybook, as many at once as there are of you, the rest waiting their turn, casting everything they ever hit you with. They're exactly as good as you remember. For the first time all summer, none of them surprises you.
  • The beat: it's the only slate with no line above yours. You chalk it. Then it's supper, and the loser lays the table, so you lay it, and you sit down.
  • On return: the window holds, and Mrs Aumbry complains about the view.

The turns

The midpoint: home for midsummer. Tradition brings every journeyman home halfway, and this year Nan Cobbold of Pennick and her brother Gaffer of Dunnock Point each decide the great harbour bell is their bank's for midsummer, so each sets their own school to moving it, three tons of bronze shoved up and down the Long Walk by spells, your whole street watching from the upstairs windows and cheering, everyone conducting into everyone, turrets on the rooftops, until the tar store catches. Every stray spell damages the Walk and the town can name who did the last of it. The Pargeters' house, nearest the tar, is gone before anyone has stopped fighting. The bell goes through the deck into the harbour, and a bolt through wet boards burns Bet's casting hand. It's the last lightning that truly crossed her, yours or Ines's, and she won't let anyone look.

The slate is that night: put it out, in three lulls, each one teaching a turret a new payload to carry Water. Both schools chalk one shared line, houses saved, and it can never be retaken. No supper.

After it, each bank trusts only its own school, so each builds out from its own shore, and Dov, frightened of water, stays to run the Dunnock end. Chair. Last Ferry, the night the boats stop for autumn, is chalked on the first pile (Gaffer has it as Boats-In), and that's your clock. Out in the harbour at night you can hear the bell tolling, somewhere new every visit, because something has climbed inside it and is wearing it. Each homecoming is a defence on the newest span, each span needs one new art (glyphs, orbitals, auras) and teaches it by needing it, devices you leave standing release their binding when you sail, and Dov's end is measured against yours and chalked. On the last of those visits you find he'd never have gone near that rail. It was a spoon. Your focus is pinning his handrail, next to his name, and he won't give it back. Bet returns casting off-hand, all Shield and Sigil, and she's better than before.

The late-game turn: the peak at Twice. Bet's waiting on the catch-stone, the whole mountain prepared, and the first bolt lands as you start, so the spar is simply who's standing there for the second, in front of the lamp-carriers and an island holding its breath, and every try goes in the cover. Halfway through she conjures a second self to be her right hand and you nearly cry, and it goes in your copybook because it touched you. Then you take the stone, the second bolt comes down through you, and every lamp on the island goes on at once, far below. No asterisk. You meant it: you took something she wanted. She laughs till she has to sit down, walks to the harbour and takes the letter, a posting a whole season's sail away that she'd said she would answer when somebody beat her. Every time you lost, you kept her. Last chair.

The ending

You come home from the Rucks to Mr Sedge on the Pennick shore with a draughts board and his first win in half a century, radiant, and Mrs Ruddock, who had it in three, studying the sky. Bet never let anyone do that for her. Nan and Gaffer, in different words, ask you to stay.

Last Ferry. Hob's brought bread, Ines sits at the far end, Dov's on his handrail, Bet has one day before her boat, and they're on the bridge with you from the first minute, all in each other's way on one deck, none covering for you. The thing comes up out of the harbour tolling, and it's a party, every turret and bolt you've ever been hit with going off around your ears, until the bell is off its back and it bolts for open water, pink and outraged.

Then the two halves of the bridge meet a yard out of true, because each side measured its own way, and the loser lays the table, so you lay it, across the gap, because there's nowhere else, and it's the last span. Every Dunnock's chair is full. The Pargeters' plot still has a rope round it, and their chair is laid and empty. Ines, not looking up, folds a page into your copybook marked if you want it: a queue, no name, the true one.

In the morning the chairs are stacked and they have all gone. You walk the bridge alone with nothing left to do. On the table, in chalk, in a left-handed scrawl: a five-rune queue you have never seen and two words, Your go.

You may stand there as long as you like. If you can read runes, you know exactly what it does. It is the game's last input. You queue it and cast it, and it picks you up and throws you off the bridge into the harbour, and it writes itself into the copybook, last entry.

Last image: you, surfacing under the crooked join, people crossing overhead with baskets, and on the underside of the table, where only someone in the water could read it, That's one to me.

After the credits the world stays open, the room at the join has a lamp in it, and every Dunnock but one is at their post getting better. Bet's lines on the slates never change again, so they can be beaten for good.

The recurring faces

  • Bet Oake. Grins hardest when losing. Wants to be beaten properly, once.
  • Hob Tansley. Apologises while casting. Wants to be asked to stay somewhere.
  • Ines Quarrie. Studies your last fight and brings the answer. Wants a problem she can't solve.
  • Little Dov Ampleforth. Enormous, afraid of water. Wants his name on something that lasts.
  • Captain Sloe. Steers with her foot and reads. Wants to know how her novel ends.
  • Mr Sedge and Mrs Ruddock. Draughts by post across a bridge they could walk in ten minutes. He wants one win. She wants the game to go on.
  • Nan and Gaffer Cobbold. Won't use each other's word for anything. Each wants the last one.
  • The Pargeters. A rope round a bare plot. They want it left alone.

Why this is fun to play

You lose to someone pressing the same keys you have, and then the spell is in your book and you're trying it on a duck. Every old region holds a number somebody keeps lowering. In joint fights you aim through friends who aim through you, and half of what you learn is where to stand. The Dunnocks are four authored repertoires that grow each region and pick by simple rules, so you come to know who cast what from its shape.

The biggest weakness

Players have to adore four people who get one line per supper, and with no doom to fall back on, thin mimicry and repertoires leave the ending a neat trick played on a stranger. The bell-wearing thing must also frighten in the dark for the whole back half, or the finale is a sketch.

The Flitting Year

Best of it
It alone extends your world rather than plotting over it: magic runs like climate, so the climate moves. Every land becomes two lands, and four thousand people on folding chairs applaud the volcano going out.
The problem
Arrive, choose, ride the front, six times over. You risk feeling like logistics, not a wizard, and it would be the most expensive of the four to build.
Read the whole story

Pitch. Once in 211 years every field in the world moves one seat around the great sea, in a known order, on a published date, and two million people, a flying school and a herd that takes nine days to pass a fixed point must move with them.

The question

Will it all be across before its field goes? The ending answers with a count: most of it, and exactly what was lost, shaped by the jobs you took and the jobs you left.

What everyone knows. Six fields sit in seven seats. The empty seat has no field on it, so the deep Arcane lies bare to the sky. In a Flit the field beside the gap slides into it, the next field follows, and so on round the sea for a year. Children learn the order from a game called Shove Along.

What a front does. A field leaves in one day, along a wall of weather that crosses its whole land. As the line passes, everything the field was holding lets go: forges burst, dams fail, ice roads open, standing crops die within the hour. Behind it lies bare Arcane, where nothing planted sets and nothing heavy stays reliably down, and a person's hands and sleep begin to fail within a season. The land takes a lifetime to follow its field. The people get one day.

What comes up. Wherever the ground lies bare, sounders rise: deep-field animals that surface and feed in the open gap as whales do at a krill bloom. They follow it around the world all year, and they grow.

What the circuit is for. Each well is ringed by groynes, basalt breakwaters ten generations in the building. A wizard attuned to the well can feed them and turn a front by a few hundred yards, which decides whether a city's granary goes under. Every cohort for 211 years has walked the circuit so that trained hands would exist when the year came. You drew the year.

The calendar. Each leg has six dated jobs and four stretches of about six days, and a job costs a stretch. Two of the six are a named pair falling due together, so one is always lost. The calendar moves only when you commit, and the host or a quick vote picks. A job you pass on falls to the class below yours, sixteen months less trained and in tremendous spirits, and returns as a harder salvage on the same map. Salvage recovers the cargo and the device, never the person. One trip back per leg is free. Each seat is one map whose bands, palette and enemies change with its field.

Growing. At every well you deepen any pool you choose and take a large regen step. Once a new field has settled, its well offers a second attunement, a bonus the main road doesn't need. Jobs pay regen fractions and new workings.

The opening

The academy is the floating tower, a mountain's worth of masonry over the eastern purple, riding at the end of chains a mile long. A mile is the height at which the deep field draws out a caster's gifts and leaves their hands alone. It floats only on bare Arcane, so it must be towed after the gap. Your last week is the great packing, and every lesson is a load: clear the rune lab, rig the chains, get the dummies down nine hundred steps. You receive your etched focus, weapons and guards only. Graduation is parting the first mooring chain with the blade it conjures. Then the road south.

The road

The order is the Flit's. Rest is the tower's roof, a plotting floor forty paces across inlaid with the continent, where you walk to your next job. On the eve of each lift the departing field stands up into the night as a wall of coloured light. The great sights play out on that floor and on painted cards. In a fight, a front is a line of light and shadow crossing your screen, one band on each side of it.

Pinned up in every port is the fen's plan: the high road through the mesa gate, opened by the Carrow key, which lies on ice with the last Flit's surveyor.

1. The South: Fire leaves first
  • Asks: bend the front seaward by feeding the groynes, Fire ahead of the line and bare Arcane behind.
  • The pair: pour the great winch for the tower's tow, feeding a crucible between waves. Or haul the skid road alongside Teague Ollerton's gang, so Wenna Tregear's forge trains, ninety anvils on runners, get out. Pass on the winch and the class below casts a flawed one. Pass on the road and Wenna is lost.
  • Fights: fire-herds, a fire-mare tall as a church, the first sounders, dog-sized.
  • The beat: the mountain has lit the southern sky for 211 years, and sailors steer by it from two hundred miles out. On the day, four thousand people set out folding chairs and a brass band. It goes dark on schedule, in a single breath, and they applaud until their hands hurt.
  • Back: a purple crater with an Arcane constraint trial that exists only until Life comes. Then green ash, as thick as the Life you carried. The black spires are mooring stacks from the last Flit, reached by Blink.
2. The South-west: Life has to be carried
  • Asks: Life goes only where living things take it. Hold the well's front open behind the last wagons, where it flips your regen hard against you.
  • The pair: bring the herds through the great arch, or string copper through the tree's crown with Hesper Gaunt before the first storm.
  • Fights: the largest escort in the game, cattle, deer, hives on poles and seed carts streaming nine days through a stone gate a thousand feet high. A pack-mother works the flanks. Barley farmers defend their fields with spells like yours.
  • The beat: the tree cannot walk. It stands higher than any cloud that crosses it, and Lightning is coming. Half the town leaves. Hesper's half climbs. If you strung the copper, a mile of tree blazes blue at the first strike and stands. If you did not, the crown burns for a month and Hesper is gone. If you left the herds, a third of them die at the arch, and the South greens thin.
  • Back: lamps on every limb or a black crown, and a shrine in the roots open only in storm season.
3. The North-west island: Lightning, and a plan
  • Asks: bleed the crystal peak's charge away groyne by groyne as the front tries to leave at once.
  • The pair: take the outer coil offline, or ferry the families off Bellwire Row.
  • Fights: chaining swarms, a storm that moves as one body, coil-yard casters, skilled and terrified.
  • The beat: the island stays. Ilse Penhallow's coil will hold a charge through 211 years of Cold, and thirty thousand people have chosen to believe her. The peak draws down the sky and grounds through the town. Whatever you did, a stretch of Bellwire Row is gone.
  • Back: the sea frozen to the mainland, a road where ferries ran. The gap in the street is still there. Ilse is rebuilding the coil and does not mention it. A shrine in one crystal face lasts as long as its light.
4. The northern ice: Cold, and the stores of the world
  • Asks: chill the road behind you as you go, attuning on the move.
  • The pair: run the last seed sledges, or cut Ottoline Brack and the key out of the monastery's ice vault.
  • Fights: convoy gauntlet over breaking ice, an armoured thing waiting under the road.
  • The beat: eleven thousand sledges carry the continent's seed and records across the ice. For most of a day the road runs through the ribs of Late, a sounder that stayed up too long at the last Flit and had Cold close over it. Its skull looks back the way it came. Every driver bawls "On time!" at it in passing, and then drives very fast.
  • Back: meltwater thundering over bare rock, Late standing clear of the ice for the first time, Earth shrines surfacing in the thaw.
5. The ochre mesas: Earth and the great lock

  • Fights: pier defences, sounders the size of churches in the outer lagoons, a blind eel whose body closes the main channel and will not be hurried.

The turns

Midpoint: the Strait, and the Sigil art. As the island's field loosens, a strip of bare Arcane opens across forty miles of open sea for nine days, and the tower crosses on it behind the winch and eleven hundred fishing boats. Ishbel Orrin's watchers row alongside in their thousands. You go down to the water, where no student is ever allowed, with the deep field under you and no land between. A chain parts. The art opens, and one forced glyph holds the line: you could only shape what you held, and now you shape the world around you. Six calm days follow on the decks, teaching turrets, glyphs and Blink, while Keziah Pask, skipper of the lead boat, teaches you the tow calls and crews race one another and sing across the gap. You attune to Arcane on the seventh. Then the strip closes a day short, Bettany cannot explain it, and the sounders come up the size of ships. The tower's west wing tears away with every old edition of the almanac. Of the tow crews, 212 do not come home, and Keziah is one, whatever you do. That is the floor. Every hull you fail to defend adds to it, and a flawed winch adds ninety more, Teague Ollerton among them. From here you know the count will have names in it.

Late turn: the far end of the bench. On the ice road, Cold lifts eleven days early and the last lifts begin to crowd together. Every child knows the far end of the bench gets shoved hardest. The first almanac carried a figure for it, and a later edition honestly tidied it away as a misprint. The stretches visibly shorten on the plotting floor, and what is offered narrows to what can still be done. On that road the monks' crews teach you the old ward-work, and Shield's wider arts open: you hold a piece of the world still while the sledges go over it.

The ending

The fen's barges and unbolted stilt-houses ride the leading edge of Water up the valley, eleven miles of moving city, and you fight along it in stages while bare Arcane runs behind you and your regen changes with every band. You attune to Water in the middle of it, the pool deepening as the well goes out beneath you. Each job you took is a device on the route that you feed: Hesper's copper arcs down the barges when charged, Wenna's furnace hulls take Fire, ice booms take Cold. The fight is tuned for about half of them. A bare post removes an option and adds no enemies. The high road is exposed, the gorge narrow, and neither is easier. Your own nova will hole a barge, whose crew swim, furious, and whose town's lamp burns lower for it.

The last sounder surfaces a mile long across the fleet's path, feeding, unaware that you exist. It never hunts. Its flank comes through a screen at a time and breaks hulls by accident, and you bend the front to swing eleven miles of city around it. It ends when the last hull clears the mesa's weir or the front catches the rest, which lowers the count and is never a game over.

Nobody saves the world, because the world is fine. The Flit completes, every colour on the map sits one seat along, and the whole continent is open to walk again.

The answer is on the plotting floor, where a lamp for each town moves to where its people ended. Where nobody arrived, there is no lamp. A new almanac goes to press. Everyone saw this year run early. Only Ottoline can say by how much at each seat, and without her the printers set this year's error as the rule.

Last image: the tower hangs at the end of its last chain, a mile above the drowned piers of the fen. On the final night all six fields stand up at once in seats nobody living has seen them in, walls of light around the entire sea. Far below in the emptied purple, forty windows are lit.

The recurring faces

  • Bettany Rook, head porter. A voice that carries two miles over water. Wants to bring the tower in with everyone she left with, and after the Strait she cannot.
  • Ottoline Brack, surveyor of the last Flit. Paces out every road she walks. Wants her figures back in the book.
  • Wenna Tregear, smith. Sings at the anvil, badly and at volume. Wants the first fire lit in the east.
  • Ishbel Orrin, leader of the Following. Has not looked at the ground in years. Wants to stand nearer each lift than anyone has, and brings thirty thousand watchers who become two hundred thousand spare hands.
  • Pim Akehurst, of the class below. Has never finished reading an instruction. Wants to be sent on something important.

Why this is fun to play

A line of changing ground crosses the fight, your regen differs on either side of it, and you bend that line with your own spells. Every pair of jobs hurts, and in the finale you are feeding the tools you earned. With friends, a moving city under attack is friendly fire at its grandest.

The biggest weakness

The true opponent is a date. The largest sounder cannot be beaten, and players who want a face get a frightened mason. The repeated shape of arrive, choose, front could also tire by the mesas.

To attune at each great well you give your word before the people who live on it, and the circuit becomes the long road back to pay them, until what you have sworn to one people you can keep only by failing another.

The question

When the circuit is walked, which gates will open to you?

The last road of the game answers it literally, passing every town you swore to, each gate as your deeds left it.

The opening

The school stands on a tidal islet in the bay beneath the southern badlands, on an Arcane vein. On your last night a boat is lost beyond the bar, and a shore-mother asks you on the school steps to bring it home. You swear aloud. A hazel stick is notched and split between you. The causeway, dry at first and then drowning, teaches the queue, the conjured weapon and the danger of your friends. At dawn both halves burn on the quay. Any opening ending in one burned stick may replace this.

The road

How the word is given. The wells stand on common ground and belong to those who live around them. They have always admitted journeymen, because a wizard in their debt is the only wizard a village can hope for. No magic binds an oath, and no attunement can be taken back. There is only what you said, before all the people.

  • Three records. Kept: both halves burn at the hearth, and gates and hearths open. Kept to the letter: your half is given back unburned, the gate opens, and no one asks you in. Broken: the town nails its half to the gate.
  • Wording. Three great oaths are offered in two wordings; the host chooses, or a quick vote. The generous wording opens that town's keeping shrine on the day you swear. The narrow one waits until the debt is paid, and leaves room later.
  • Instalments. Each great oath has a smaller due on return. At most two stand open at once. A well taken while one is open cuts a notch: the debt grows by one encounter, and that town's shrine closes until it is paid. The journeymen's boats make any region one crossing away.
  • Small oaths. Two or three to a village across some fifteen villages, built from the shrine kits as restrictions and tasks: no Lightning in the mill valley, for the sake of a goose of great age. Each village has one face and one gate.
  • Power. Only the ford and the last Hearing split the paths, and each offers the same total power either way.

Each attunement grants a regen step and deepens the pool you choose. The order is fixed, for debts must be scheduled. Return paths accept two answers or a regen step.

1. Ighrem n'Aman (Earth), the mesas
  • Place: a fortress, its cisterns cut so deep that stars show in the water at noon.
  • Oath: "When the cisterns fail, bring water to us," or "bring us to water." They have not failed in two hundred years, and the people cheer as at a wedding.
  • Exam: drive the burrowers from the well shaft.
  • Growth: walls you can afford to keep raising in a long fight.
  • The beat: Tiziri lets down the plumb line and reads the depth aloud. Below the fortress, children play on a ruined stair running north toward the fen, carved with the names of those who climbed it.
  • On return: the reading is painted on the lift wall, lower at each visit. Rain banked in the cisterns is the instalment and raises the number. Tiziri says at once what Rain can do: it buys seasons, and will never fill the cisterns.
2. Aukmedis (Life), the great forest
  • Place: a tree so vast it makes its own rain. The parish lays its dead among the root-halls, and from each grave a green shoot rises the same spring.
  • Oath: "Never bring fire past the ford, and lay Shushan at the roots when her day comes." A Fire spell in ford country counts only if something is still burning and someone could see it. If nobody saw it burning, you never did it.
  • Exam: escort the root-tenders through the halls, against pests that beg to be burned.
  • Growth: healing you can sustain between fights.
  • The beat: Shushan, eighty years old, holds the ford and searches your pockets for flint. Hers is a southern name among the people of the tree.
  • On return: she is slower each time and misses nothing. The instalment is a nest turned out of the root-halls without flame.
3. Ulan Khalga (Lightning), the red badlands
  • Place: a great arch of stone. On storm nights a single bolt stands in the span for minutes together, and the herders swear by its light.
  • Oath: "Stand for our side when the boundary is heard." The boundary is the summer pasture against Aukmedis, to whom you swore last season.
  • Exam: cross the open ground in storm, among swarms that chain.
  • Growth: chains you can repeat.
  • Encounters: steppe ambushes, and your first enemy casters, claim-jumpers fighting for a living.
  • The beat: Odval explains the Hearing. Each side's sworn wizard stands on the line beneath the bolt until one has stepped off. The herders' gate bears a nailed stick in the name of your classmate Ilarion Kask, the first broken word you see.
  • On return: the first due is a wedding. By herders' custom the two families' guests assail one another with all they possess until the bride's mother cries peace, and it is held a discourtesy to leave a friend unburnt. It is the happiest day of the circuit. The bride sets you alight, and the chronicle records it.
4. Rawa Tanggul (Water), the fen
  • Place: a city on stilts behind a dyke raised by more forebears than anyone can name.
  • Oath: "Come back and hold the dyke when the spring melt comes," or "come back and stand on the dyke."
  • Exam: a flooded gauntlet that teaches conduction without mercy.
  • Growth: Rain you can keep falling across a whole encounter.
  • Encounters: amphibious packs, a native boss in the great sluice.
  • The beat: the people walk you the length of the dyke, each family naming its stretch. On the oldest piles are the names from the mesa's stair.
  • On return: by the melt, the mesa's first households are camped outside the dyke, in twos and threes.
5. Krakadzor (Fire), the volcanic south
  • Place: a city of smiths cut into the crater's inner wall. Its dead go to the vent. They hold that those left unburned go on walking home.
  • Oath: Zabel, Shushan's sister, asks it: "Bring our sister home to the fire."
  • Exam: cross the caldera floor between two breathings of the lava, on a crust your own Fire will break.
  • Growth: burning you can maintain.
  • The beat: Zabel shows you fifty years of gifts laid by for a homecoming. You carry Zabel's half to the ford on your belt. Shushan's hands go through your pockets, find it, read the notches, and put it back. She lets you pass.
  • On return: the instalment is one more length of the crater road cleared for a woman who has not yet come.
6. Minar-e Shab (Arcane), the warped east
  • Place: a black tower hanging unsupported above the well, its shadow falling toward the sun. Each year someone must climb and watch one night. No chronicle says what is watched. Each year a child is chosen by lot.
  • Oath: "Stand this year's watch in place of Golnar."
  • Exam: the ascent through rooms that hold your own footprints ahead of you, then the night's defence of the topmost chamber.
  • Growth: the largest regen step in the game, enough to hold the deep beam.
  • Encounters: strange things, few and terrible; masterful enemy casters.
  • The beat: you come down at dawn and a full year has passed. Golnar, a head taller, meets you at the foot of the tower. She has had the year you spent.
  • On return: the instalment is clearing the lower rooms before the next child climbs.
7. Davgi (Cold), the ice
  • Place: the ribs of something no singer has named rise from the ice higher than any tower built by hands, and a people live in their shelter. For centuries their singers have held every oath of the circuit by heart. Claims too great for any arch are heard here, on the ice between the ribs.
  • Oath: "Whatever you decide, you will say it aloud before the singers, and change no word of what they sing."
  • Exam: a long ice road against large and patient things.
  • Growth: ice and shatter you can use twice in any fight.
  • The beat: Máret sings you the circuit so far, each oath in your own wording.

Optional: the crystal island, north-west. Wizards who swore never to swear again teach Shield's wider arts, the will to refuse.

The turns

The midpoint, and the opening of Sigil. The melt and the Hearing fall due in one week at opposite ends of the world. There is one boat and one party.

On the voyage you put in at Sev Karer, the forest of black columns, where the cutters carve stones that go on working when the carver has gone home. Hovik sets you a trial against a small practice flood. The full art opens, and your Sigil pool rises to three: turrets, glyphs, Blink, auras. "You have shaped what you hold. Shape the wall."

Floes ram the dyke and eel-packs climb the breaches. Only carved work stays, so turrets take the packs and Earth and Cold go in as glyph payloads against the ice. The stretches scale to the devices you hold. Now you choose.

Stay. You fight the whole melt at the families' sides, the greatest battle of the first half, and every house stands. The stick burns and every hearth opens. The herders' half is nailed up, and the parish's flocks graze their pasture bare.

Sail. You build first, family by family. Every device you leave holds a Sigil rune from you, so whatever you give the dyke is missing from your duel. At the Hearing you face Ilarion Kask, sworn to Aukmedis and glad to see you. The party holds the line until the last of you steps off it, and the oath is kept win or lose. Win, and the parish loses its summer grazing.

Then you sail back for the last night and find the dyke exactly as your layout left it. Stretches that failed cost those families their houses, visibly. If too many fell, or if you swore to stand on the dyke, the stick is nailed. If enough held, Kemala Rawi gives you back your half unburned. She says only, "You left."

The boatmen set the price of the warped river, the only way east: "No one shall settle the dyke lands," or "no one shall settle them against the will of those who hold them." You have seen the camps. You have read the names on the stair. You swear, or you never reach the sixth well.

The late turn: Shushan's day. You come down from the tower into the year you lost. Every open stick has taken a notch. Shushan has died. She lies in the root-halls through the forty days of watching, and Krakadzor's fire-bearers are on the road.

  • Carry her home. Escort her bier through a parish that bars your way. You swear on the bank to shed no parish blood, so everyone you fell must be raised by Life before you may cross. The ford's gate takes the stick, and the tree roads close. At the vent, Zabel opens the gifts and with them fifty years of letters from Shushan, each promising to come home next spring. To the parish, she swore she never would. This is learned only here.
  • Lay her at the roots. The fire-bearers and their sworn casters come to the ford with torches. You hold it without Fire, for you swore that also. Krakadzor's gate takes the stick, the upper halls open, and in spring a shoot rises.

The ending

Ighrem's reading comes to nothing. Two thousand people go down the old stair and walk north, carrying their children, and halt at the fen's border stones. Kemala gives the numbers once: the dyke lands feed so many, and a third more would starve every household in a bad year. Neither people lies. A claim so great can be heard in one place only.

You go north with the sworn of both peoples. On the ice at Davgi you step to the line and say aloud, before the singers, whom you stand with.

The last Hearing is fought across the seven spans between the ribs. The line moves one span with each side driven back. Every caster across the line uses your own language, and you know their faces.

  • Your deeds decide the field. Each great town you kept sends its sworn to your side, and each removes one threat from one span. Those you failed stand across from you.
  • Ilarion Kask holds the last span. They swore to both peoples and stayed with the one you left.
  • Banked Rain decides how many of the column reached the stones with strength to go on, whichever way the verdict falls.

If you stand with Rawa Tanggul, the column is turned toward the southern strand. If you swore "bring us to water," you may walk them there yourself, and Ighrem gives your half back unburned. If you stand with the mesa-folk, they are brought in, and the old families grow crowded and hungry. If you swore "against the will of those who hold them," and enough families whose houses stood give leave, the city gives your half back unburned. Failing that, the stick is nailed. One stick burns and one never does. The emptied cisterns of Ighrem open as a deep new road.

The singing is played. You stand on the ice, and verse by verse every oath is sung to the faces it was said to, in your wording, with what came of it. Each town answers by burning its half, or holding it up. "You left" is sung to the city that heard it. At the ford, the singer records only that nobody saw anything, and the parish looks at you. The wedding is sung, and both peoples laugh, and for that verse no one is on any side. At any verse you may stop the song. That verse goes unsung, and Davgi nails your stick.

Then comes the long road home, past each gate.

Last image: you stop at the closed place of the people you chose against, a walkway drawn in or a rope across the strand path. A shutter opens. It belongs to the family whose stretch of the dyke held, or whose terrace you filled with Rain. They look at you for a long while. They set out a bowl on the last post, and the shutter closes. If you left them nothing to be grateful for, the shutter stays closed. You eat, or you do not, and you go on with the unburned half at your belt.

The recurring faces

  • Máret, singer of Davgi. Hears everything once. Walks the circuit behind you to get your words exact.
  • Ilarion Kask, of your class. Gives their word as others give greetings. Wants to be loved by everyone.
  • Shushan, at the ford. Her hands go through every pocket. Wants to be spared the choosing.
  • Tiziri n'Aman, of the cisterns. Thirty-one years at the plumb line. Wants the last reading to fall to someone else.

Why this is fun to play

You return to places where you were weak and settle in moments of well-chosen runes what you once swore to settle someday. Your oaths reach into your hands: a ford held against torches with Fire forbidden, a friend's mistake doused before anyone can see it, a wedding where setting your comrades alight is good manners. Each rune you leave on the dyke is one you lack in the duel, so preparation and promise are one decision.

The biggest weakness

The seven great oaths are required, and a sharp player will see the last Hearing coming hours ahead and may resent being made to swear. Foresight changes how the losers lose and never who loses.

The question

Cover sheet, two lines. Who is he. Who let him drown. A third gets added in his own handwriting at the Ice Road: Who wound the gate.

Closed as: Joost Tolhuis, gateman at the Sea Gate, blamed for 163 drowned in Low Hask, 22 years back. Gate wound by his apprentice, aged 17, to float her father's barge. Plank rotted on its own. She stood at the rail and counted ten before she shouted. Greet Vrolijk, builder of the town that replaced the one she drowned. Sold you boots at cost.

The opening

School: river mouth, Arcane vein, edge of the purple land. Half a day upriver, unseen from here, the Sea Gate, which keeps the tide out of the fen. Last chore for leavers: clear the slip. Today it holds a body. Swappable. Teaches one rune each through handling him: thaw the coat, keep crabs off, conjure a blade to cut the pockets, water the plant.

Found on him. Male, about 70, right hand three fingers short, old injury.

  • A. Grafting knife, pear cutting in wet moss
  • B. Brass signal key
  • C. Pit check, no. 77
  • D. Iron door key
  • E. Half a sheared bronze pin
  • F. Ice cleats, storehouse chit
  • G. Basket token

First reading. Moss still dry at the heart: in the water under a day. Packed under all seven remaining nails: tarred oak, rotten. He held on to something for a long while, somewhere with piles. Nobody came. Hence line two.

G. Vrolijk, on the slip, collecting piling iron, came down on the same tide: river's shut above the school till spring, for piling. Nobody's getting up it. Offers boots at cost for the road.

Reeve Sole: body goes on ice in the fishmonger's cold-house. Fishmonger objects, on record. "You're going round anyway."

The road

Method. Any spell works on any prop, always. Zap key B on the slip: it stutters —DUE —AD —ING. Locals teach the trick; wells only give depth (one pool of your choice, regen step). Cast changes the answer: Force reads one line deep, Area reads the room and ruins half of it, Place reads the surface it lands on. Late props chain: frost, then relight what the frost exposed. Every key prop takes three spell routes in different elements, so any build gets through; a witness is the fallback. Shrines give extra deepenings. Evidence burns. Your friend's stray Lightning counts.

Keeping trials: evidence under threat, rising water or spreading fire. Solo answer: ward first, read second.

Clock. The forecast moves only at story beats.

Each land has a local wrong at the edge, a harder one in the heartland, and the well as exam. Creatures dig up, eat or nest in evidence. Orchard and Island in either order; then fixed.

Orchard Country (south-west, Life).

  • The tree: flowers one night, in no season anyone can predict. Every graft ever taken from it flowers the same night, wherever it stands.
  • Attunement: graft a scion to its root and keep it alive one night.
  • Trick: Life on soil raises seedlings along what's buried; on timber, brings up the rings.
  • Local wrong: somebody moved a boundary hedge. Moles the size of dogs undermine the old stakes before you can read them.
  • Exhibit A: here he was Perrin, hired hand. Bred a pear, the Lockstreet. Paid one orphan's apprenticeship.
  • Statements: orphan, now 30: "Very tall." Three neighbours: "Short." Orphan was eight.
  • Return, changed statement: she knew who he was at 16 and wrote to Low Hask survivors with his whereabouts. Strongbox under the rows, with what's nesting on it.

Spark Island (north-west, Lightning).

  • Attunement: carry a charge up the crystal peak and earth it. The peak strikes upward, at whatever on the slope holds most charge, and well-band regen keeps filling you. At the top the crystal plays back every storm it has taken, all at once.
  • Trick: Lightning into a dead device repeats its last act.
  • Local wrong: money orders going astray. Swarms chew the line.
  • Exhibit B: as Line-Dirk he walked the wire nine years. Key, fully woken: TIDE DUE. PIN BAD. COMING.
  • Forecast, station log: a shelf the size of a county has broken off the north ice and will ground at the river mouth at the harvest tide. One surge, once.
  • Operator: nobody touches her set. Asked who sent that message: nobody. Asked who sent it nobody: "Him."
  • Return: Force through the main relay prints every message he sent. Mostly money orders. One line of the addressee list razored out. Receipt: G. Vrolijk bought wire here four days before you came. Changed statement: she did touch the set. He paid her to.

The Mesa (north-centre, Earth).

  • The fortress: four hundred years of defences, every arrow-slit pointing inward and down. It has never been attacked from outside.
  • Attunement: hold the breach at the annual drill, when what lives in the shaft comes up. It has come every year.
  • Trick: Earth lifts or packs rubble so you can see under it.
  • Local wrong: Pit 9 collapse, six men never recovered. Burrowers tunnelling through the fall.
  • Exhibit C: he was Sill, paymaster of the pit burial club. Left eight years ago with the chest. Widows got nothing.
  • Statements: widow Daan: "Thief." Pit foreman: "Thief, and I'd lend him money." Follow the chest: same money the orphan got. The kindness was stolen.
  • Return: settle Pit 9 under a keeping trial. Six out. Widows paid from his strongbox, if you bring it. Changed statement: Daan went to the river last autumn. "To look at him."

The Fen (north-east, Water; river opens at this beat).

  • On the way: through the Sea Gate. Cliffs either side, bronze older than Hask, Greet's breakwater of piles out in front, unfinished. She shows you the crank herself: low, two hands. "It sticks."

  • Trick: freeze a breach with Water and Cold, bail, soak dried ink until it lifts.
  • Local wrong: salvagers selling Low Hask doorknobs. "They were given."

The Burning South (Fire).

  • The land: lava run through stone gutters, four generations of it. One street has been taken twice and rebuilt twice, same doors, same numbers.
  • Attunement: when the swell flares, as it does most years, burn the firebreak above the town before the lava gets there. Don't be standing in it.
  • Trick: careful Fire glows the ink on char, shows flaws inside metal.

  • Exhibit E: gas bubble in the pin. Founder, first visit: "Impossible." Second: "It was a busy year."

  • Order book, burnt, relit: one pin, pattern corrected, ordered by wire from the Gate station, the evening he drowned. Greet's statement: hadn't seen him in 22 years. He reached her alive.
  • Return: recast the pin, foundry defence. Black spires: constraint shrine.

Ice Road (far north, Cold).

  • The skeleton: ribs 61 paces high, standing clear of the ice. The north brings its dead here by sledge and lays them in rows between the ribs, faces whole. Families come at midwinter with food. One grandmother is still 40.
  • Attunement: walk out past the skull and stand still one night while things that wait come looking.
  • Trick: chill a surface; frost forms wherever hands gripped, and deepens with how long.

The turns

Midpoint (the Fen). Key D opens a door on Lock Street, drowned quarter. Bail it. Name on the lintel. Up in the square, the stone: 163 names and, cut small at the foot, Joost Tolhuis, who was drunk at his gate.

G. Vrolijk, the moment the name is out: "I was his apprentice. I was at my aunt's that night." Before anyone asks. Later, unprompted: "He left me alone with it. Seventeen, a tide night, and he went to a christening." True. He did.

Every land reopens.

  • The Lock Street Men, survivors who recite the names yearly, hunt you for asking. Hedge-casters, your spell language, poorly, often at each other. First fight on the road back: they ward one another, you read it off their rune pips, your hands have it. Shield's wider arts, off a suspect.
  • Cas Hoekstra, their head, confesses. Method: strangled. Told body drowned: "Strangled underwater." Stands as the working answer until a tavern slate in the Burning South puts him two lands away buying the house a round "on account of what I did."

Host names a suspect to the Reeve, once. Wrong costs one visible change in that person's land: orphan's crop unpicked, the wire down. Greet named early means rows of piles lost, and every row is ground for turrets at the end.

Sigil (Lock Street, at leisure). Low Hask had a lamp-charmer, Aaltje Roos, name 88 on the stone. With the water coming she etched glyphs up both sides of the stairs. They still hold air in the drowned houses. Read in sequence they give the order the street flooded: river end under at dusk, an hour before the tide. First crack in "drunk at his gate". To date a mark you need a specimen beside it. Dry landing, no hurry:

  • Specimen 1: blade, in hand. Useless.
  • Specimens 2 to 6: the same, with language.
  • Specimen 7: on the far wall, four paces from anyone, stroke order matching hers. Every focus present worn smooth.

Glyphs and turrets from here. Further down, on later bailings: her Blink marks, which took children past a flooded landing; then at the stair foot the Double, her own figure, left so they would climb away from it. Each later land gets a verb from this ladder.

Late-game turn (the tower). Beam through Piet's sealed sea-chest. Barge log, last entry: "Aground at dusk. G. says twenty minutes." Under it, his room's first receipts: paid by money order, Line-Dirk. That is the razored line. Then the first row of her breakwater: same money. The widows' chest bought her father's room and her breakwater's first piles, from a man who never knew, and she cut out the proof.

Piet tells it differently each visit. Each names something testable.

  • "Her aunt's shawl." Low Hask: aunt's day-book, ink lifted. "G. did not come. Cake uneaten."
  • "Rope burned her hands." The crank's frost shows the burn's shape.
  • One line never moves: She floated me off.

Joost heard that for nine springs and wrote "means the tide". One wizard, one Cold rune.

The ending

Harvest tide. The Sea Gate, where the fen river breaks out through the cliffs at the purple land's edge, pieces of the cliff rising past you. Regen bends Arcane, the wrong way for this job.

The walk, before the surge, played. Greet carries the lantern down a catwalk span she has not crossed since; the crew say she goes round. Stump of plank: Life brings up the rings, rot, nobody's fault. Frost on its edge: a hand of two fingers, four minutes deep. Soot on the rail, relit: a lantern ring. She sets her lantern down in it. Frost the rail, Cold depth 1 will do. Hold the spell and stand still: one grip, unmoved, comes up at ten seconds. Double where he fell. Rail within arm's reach. He'd come to warn her about the pin and ask her, of all people, who to hire. Sneek's entry, amended. "I'll tell you after the tide."

The surge. Long preparation. Seat the recast pin, one Arcane reading through the housing with water at your chest. Frost the gate, wall the breakwater, turrets on every row of piles you still have. Fight what comes in ahead of the ice. Lock Street Men on the piles, setting each other alight. Greet on the crank, because it sticks.

After. She reads your page. Checks it twice. "Ten. That's right." You hand her the cutting and his tag: For G. She counts the leaves, twice, and hands it back.

The casebook takes two lines, host or quick vote.

  • THE GATE: G.V. wound it. His name goes up among the 163; hers is known; the walkways stay and people walk them looking down. Or Stands as cut. He left a child on a tide night and his own box says "Mine."
  • THE PLANK: She counted. The Reeve comes, and next year a loose board goes uncounted. Or Rot. Also true. She stays. The gate stays sound.

The cutting goes on his grave above the slip. She watches it go in. Piet: you may bring him down, if you bail his house first. Tower record says a week.

Last entry. Cold-house returned to fishmonger. They stand at the empty slab, hat off, count of ten, hat on, start shovelling herring.

Why this is fun to play

Your hands do the detecting: each cast and mix makes the same burnt desk say something else, and the room you cleared twelve hours ago has a lead in it now you can hold a steadier Fire. Solo, you ward the cellar and read against rising water; together, one of you frosts a door handle while another fights near it with a spell that could very well reach the door handle. Going back pays twice, a stronger reading and a witness whose story has changed.

The biggest weakness

The sharp player reads the boots at the Ice Road and knows, with a land and a half still to play. Those hours run on how, on the money, and on what you'll write. And the file is large for its team: every land needs a local wrong, a changed statement and destroyable props.

How different they really are

The overlap checker's final verdict: four distinct games in four distinct voices, with small collisions left over. The first drafts had very different premises but read like one author, with the same deflated landmarks, the same dry voice and the same water finale, so each writer was given a separate voice and a private list of things to replace. Two habits survived: grand landmarks with one uncanny rule, and in the second round every last fight happening at the fen. Three of the four finales were moved for the final draft.

All the writers and judges are Claude models, and the four starting angles were chosen by Claude, so a fifth kind of story you'd have thought of won't be here. The editors' notes and overlap reports are in docs/explorations/2026-11-09-story/.
